Like [<em>London Falling</em> by Patrick Radden Keefe](london-falling.html), this is an investigative true-crime nonfiction. Jon Ronson is a British-American journalist. His books <em>The Psychopath Test</em> (2011) and <em>So You’ve Been Publicly Shamed</em> (2015) are quite popular.

When a nonfiction reads like a fiction, and starts with a mystery, you know you are booked for a few days of undisturbed reading, and that’s exactly what I did.

The book opens with the author’s 21-year-old son, Joel, sending him messages, a picture and coordinates of the place he had been one night in 2023 while the author was sleeping. Joel had never done that before, and the picture was quite unusual. The author got intrigued and started his investigation.

That night, Joel and his friends were invited to a party. When they followed the map to reach the destination, they were astonished and a little petrified by what they saw. In front of them was a vast medieval castle in Connecticut, owned by one Chris Mark, a man aged around sixty. As the investigation progressed, the author unfolded the bizarre lifestyle of Chris Mark.

Chris Mark had inherited lots of money and built the castle. He wanted to live a fantasy life like Hugh Hefner. He lured young women to come and live with him in the castle and to act like his princesses. He planned to create a reality show where these women would win his hand. In return, he promised them gifts, money, and help building a better future. Though he said that there was no sex involved, he would pester them for sex all the time.

The author then went on to find other men who were not normal and had lived eccentric lives.

Greg Chism started as a lawn-care influencer on YouTube. As he got a taste of success, he started making videos of his daughters, which were innocent in the beginning. But later he claimed that he was under so much pressure to earn incentives from YouTube, his videos spiralled into becoming crass.

Graham Linehan, the author’s long-time friend, became obsessed with the need to fight against absolute evil that he thought others were. He became impolite, mannerless and angry.

Andrew Gold — to grow his audience, he made divisive content and posed himself as a victim of “wokeness.”

What the author is really trying to do in this book is to find parallels between these men’s lives and show how, over the years, men are finding themselves in difficult situations. He then brings up the idea of “missing men”. Men who are not working, studying or have any purpose in life. They are acting out in uncanny ways or completely checked out from normal lives. They are lost and sometimes misunderstood, even. He calls it “crisis of masculinity.”

Though I enjoyed reading it, I don’t really agree with the conclusions the author draws. Trying to tie together the stories of these men, who, according to him, are acting out because they are living lives without purpose, I feel, was far-fetched. I didn’t really see the connections between these stories, and putting them all under one umbrella was not convincing. Sometimes, it felt repetitive and dragging, even.

It is still a good read for Jon’s storytelling, his reactions and reflections, which you will find throughout the book and the empathy he has towards the subjects.
