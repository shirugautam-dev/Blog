Taylor Jenkins Reid is an American author, and Atmosphere is her ninth novel. After reading [<em>Evidence of the Affair</em>](evidence-of-the-affair.html), a novella by her, I knew I had to read Atmosphere. Everyone was raving about it. I waited some time before I could start, as I already had three books running at the time, and I also wanted it to be a treat that I saved for last. But sadly, this one did not turn out as I expected. Maybe I expected too much.

The opening is arguably the most intense scene with high stakes, which should have had me in its grip, but it didn’t. Technical detail was too much; it almost felt like a science class. Too many characters were introduced at once; it was difficult to remember them. A space mission was failing, and people were dying, but without getting to know them properly, I couldn’t feel the tension.

I kept on reading, hoping to feel the emotions the author was trying to evoke in readers. There was none to come. At about 40% of the book, I even asked ChatGPT when it was going to be interesting. It told me that it is slow in the beginning; later it becomes intense. I finished the whole book never feeling that way even once.

It’s a story of two women, Joan Goodwin and Venessa Ford, who fall in love at a time when their love is not accepted.

The plot follows two different times: the 1980s and December 1984. The novel starts with the catastrophic event in December 1984, when Vanessa goes to space, and Joan is a CAPCOM, and then it goes back to the 1980s, during NASA’s Space Shuttle program training period.

In 1980, out of thousands of applicants, Joan is chosen to be an astronaut. She is single, brilliant and kind. During her training, she meets other equally brilliant astronauts and pilots. One of them is Vanessa, whom Joan thinks is mysterious. As training progresses, they get to know each other better. Joan finds love and passion she never believed she was capable of. They struggle to hide their relationship in the sexist, male-dominated environment.

<em>“Bravery is being unafraid of something other people are afraid of. Courage is being afraid, but strong enough to do it anyway.”</em>

Joan also has a sister, Barbara, a single mother, who neglects her daughter, Frances. Joan steps in whenever she can. During the course of the story, Frances gets sad, distraught and alone. Ultimately, Joan takes her in and becomes her guardian.

I did not feel the spark in the relationship between Joan and Vanessa, nor the tension of them getting caught. Frances and Barbara are portrayed well. I could easily hate Barbara, which I believe the author intended, and felt bad for Frances.

All the time I felt something was missing. The scenes felt repetitive and predictable. The plot never picked up the speed. Dialogues felt like lessons about the sky, stars, the universe, life and God, and they often felt flat. Lydia’s character adds a little spice, and I expected there would be some twist, but nothing happened. The story has no highs and lows. It ends with something very foreseeable.

I wanted to like this book, and I almost felt sorry for myself at the end. After all, I am her fan.

I will read her other books, especially The Seven Husbands of Evelyn Hugo for sure, but this one is an easy miss.
