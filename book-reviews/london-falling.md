I came to know about Patrick Radden Keefe from David Perell’s podcast How I Write around six months ago. He was on my reading list. Then a couple of weeks back, I watched him in another podcast by PBS NewsHour where this book was discussed. It was like an alarm set off for me, and I read it right away.

I already had high expectations, and the book did not disappoint. It was from page one, a page-turner. The more I read, the more I wanted to go on.

It was an eye-opener in many senses. London is, apparently, not only a city with Big Ben and Buckingham Palace. It is also a city built on foreign money and manipulated by the underworld. It is also a city where ultra-rich invest in ultra-luxury apartments that sit unoccupied and dark most of the time. It is also a city that has changed itself from docks and factories to a safe deposit vault for unexplained wealth. The city has a history of drug-fuelled violence, speculatively risen real estate values, fixers that help offshore shell companies launder money and institutions that are ready to turn a blind eye. Crimes happened in plain sight while police couldn’t or wouldn’t do anything.

It is also a city where a teenager feels inadequate. He sees the money and glamour and wants to be part of that kind of world; hence, he spins stories and tells lies to get inside. He makes promises that he cannot keep, and one day he suddenly dies, and his parents come to know about the life he had lived outside the home.

The book starts with a haunting tale of a 19-year-old teenager, Zac Brettler, who was born and brought up in London. He came from a well-known and well-to-do family and had loving parents. But somehow, that was not enough.

In November 2019, he went missing. For a few days, the parents didn’t know where he was. They frantically called his friends, but no one knew. After four days, his body was found on the bank of the Thames, in front of a high-end apartment complex, Riverwalk. Police thought he died somewhere else and the body ended up there. But the cameras at the MI6 building had caught him jumping from a fifth-floor balcony of the apartment and falling into the river. When the investigation into his death started, his parents came to know that he was living a double life. He was obsessed with wealth, status and gangster movies. He wanted to live an ultra-rich life. He was associating with dangerous people and told them he was a Russian Oligarch’s son. He told them that money was coming his way and promised to invest in their businesses.

He had been lying and getting away with it for some time. In his school, neighbourhood and to other acquaintances, he had some other stories going on. To some, he said his mother was dead; to some, he said his father was dead, and his mother lived in Dubai.

A few months before his death, he met two people, Akbar Shamji and Verinder Sharma “Indian Dave”. Akbar Shamji was a dubious businessman, and Verinder was a violent debt collector who was notorious for inflicting pain with a hot knife.

Verinder Sharma was living in that apartment, and Zac had lived with him for a few months in the summer. On the night of his death, Zac, Akbar, Verinder and his daughter Dominic were in the apartment.

The parents were desperate to find the reasons and circumstances of the death. Police were adamant that it was a suicide. Though he jumped into the river, the parents thought it could not be. They sensed that there was a greater threat inside the apartment. By jumping, he was trying to save himself.

The parents were not happy with the police investigation. They started pressing for more information and hired a private detective. But by then it was too late. There were loopholes in the investigation. The police had not interviewed all the key people; they had not pressed those enough whom they interrogated and some evidence was also lost.

A few months later, Verinder Sharma also died, presumably of a drug overdose.

Zac, who pretended to be rich and Verinder, who pretended to be his mentor, were both imposters caught up in the lies they created for themselves. In the end, they both couldn’t survive.

The author writes a compelling narrative that reads like fiction. He portrays not only the immediate event, but also the world that manifested it. He brings all his research findings and notes to make a story that is easy to follow and feels personal to the readers.

The ending doesn’t give the answer to Zac’s death. We still don’t know what really happened to Zac on that night. It leaves us with the uncertainty of the world outside and makes us feel empathetic towards the parents who have to live with it.

<em>“The truth about parenthood, he concludes, is that it ‘abruptly catapults us into a permanent relationship with a stranger.”</em>

It is a book that needs to be read by all parents. It shows how parents are usually the last people to know their kids truly. Being a mother myself of a 22-year-old, this book really hit hard.
