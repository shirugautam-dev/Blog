Arundhati Roy is a well-known name in India as an author and an activist. We hear her speak on human rights and environmental issues every now and then. I read her <em>The God of Small Things</em>, a 1997 Booker Prize winner, a few years back and liked it. I was expecting this memoir to be as good. But what I didn’t expect was a roller-coaster ride. Once I started, I couldn’t put it down. I couldn’t stop talking about it with friends for a few days.

“Have you read Mother Mary Comes to Me?” became my conversation starter.

I had no idea about her background, which also made it an excellent read. I thought she was born with a silver spoon in her mouth. God no!

The author shows us how she became what she is today. Her journey from Kerala to Delhi, from an architect to a writer and from writer to an activist, is nothing short of a feat. Her complicated relationship with her mother is the backbone of the book, perhaps of her life too. She calls her <em>“my shelter and my storm”</em>.

It is sad and amusing at the same time. One minute you will be shocked by a mother’s wrath, and another minute you will be laughing out loud because the author makes light of the wretched incidents.

<em>“The world was too ridiculous for me to remain too sad for too long.”</em>

Arundhati Roy takes us to her childhood and introduces her mother, Mrs Roy (Mary Roy), a single mother who couldn’t be defeated by life. She started Pallikoodam School in Kerala and educated young minds to free themselves from societal expectations. In the author’s words, she <em>“gave them spines, she gave them wings, she set them free.”</em>

She had to fight her family to get what she believed was rightfully hers. She took her brother to the highest court of India and, in the course, helped change the inheritance laws for Christian women across Kerala.

But she also inflicted physical and emotional abuse on her children. She was prone to intense rages and ruled her household like a tyrant. The author mentions that the home, which should be a place of safety, felt dangerous and hostile. The children were hurt and oppressed. The woman who helped other children grow, be independent and free, despised her daughter when she sought the same.

<em>“When it came to me, Mrs Roy taught me how to think, then raged against my thoughts. She taught me to be free and raged against my freedom. She taught me to write and resented the author I became.”</em>

Roy ran away from home, but her brother stayed back. We, readers, are dismayed and ask ourselves if Mary Roy could be forgiven. But the author does exactly that; she loves her even.

<em>“I left home not because I didn’t love her, but so that I could continue to love her.”</em>

It is not all sad.

Her absent father enters into her adult life and adds flavour to the plot. Roy meets him after 23 years. Having separated when she was two years old, she doesn’t remember him. She feels like meeting a stranger, quiet and awkward. But now, she understands her father not from her mother’s image formed by her anger, but as someone with his own struggles.

We see the author attaining great success against all odds, and we cheer for her. The resilience she developed fighting her mother’s brutality prepares her to fight social injustice. Success doesn’t corrupt her; instead, she uses her fame to defy power and authorities.

Through this memoir, Roy reminds us how we are shaped by our childhood, our relationships, and how we are ready to forget and forgive our troubled past, for our own sake. It is also about loving those who hurt us, living with our memories and making peace with our wounds.

Roy writes beautifully with unusual candour. She softly delivers the scathing sarcasm, and we feel the pain she felt. Her brutal honesty, the willingness to bare raw, is remarkable.

It is a read one should not miss.
